Does Trump naming a company actually move its stock?

Almost never
more than noise

This is the exhaustive test. An LLM read all 23,992 Trump Truth Social posts and flagged every publicly-traded company mentioned by name, brand, executive, or ticker — no keyword shortcuts. That yields 1,055 in-market-hours post–company pairs; 819 (from 726 distinct posts, across 62 tickers) had 1-minute price data on both sides of the post. For each we measure the absolute move of the stock versus the last price before the post — does it move at all, in either direction.

The directional view (praise up, attack down)

The first minute is
a coin flip

This is the earlier, directional cut, kept for detail: it signs each move in the direction the post pushed — praise up, attack down — so a positive number means the stock moved the way the post argued. It originally ran on n = 73 observations (60 distinct posts). The median first-minute move is +0.00%, and only 48% of posts moved the right way in that minute — no better than chance. The mean move does reach +0.139% by +30 min (70% hit rate), but it accumulates gradually — most of it appears between +5 and +15 min, not on impact.

Superseded by the section above. This chart originally covered only 73 of the 179 in-hours pairs, because Polygon's free tier stops at ~2 years and older ticker-days couldn't be priced. That gap is now closed — the older bars were backfilled from Twelve Data, and an exhaustive LLM sweep expanded the universe to 819 measured pairs across 62 tickers (the "Almost never more than noise" section above). The conclusion held: naming a company moves it no more than a random minute. This directional view is retained for the praise-vs-attack breakdown.

First-minute move — the distribution behind the average

Each post's move during the minute it was posted, signed in the post's direction. The spread is what the mean hides: only 48% of posts moved the right way — a coin flip — and the median is +0.00%, i.e. the typical post moves the stock nothing at all in its first minute. The +0.041% mean is carried by a handful of outliers, not by consistency. Range -0.51% to +1.14%, SD 0.196%. n = 73.

Speed of capture — how much is already there

Mean move at each horizon as a share of the mean move at +30 min. This is not a monotonic build-up: the small first-minute blip (29%) partly fades by +5 min (21%) before the move actually accumulates between +5 and +15 min. These are ratios of two very small means (+0.041% over +0.139%), so treat the percentages as a shape, not a precise claim. n = 73.

Method & honest limitations. Source: post text/timestamps from the Matt Stiles Trump Truth Social Archive (CC0); prices from Polygon.io 1-minute aggregates, regular hours only. Sample: 179 of the dataset's praise/attack post–company pairs landed Mon–Fri 9:30–16:00 ET; 73 could be priced (no data: 102, incomplete window: 4). Effective n is smaller than 73. Three separate reasons: (1) the 73 observations come from only 60 distinct posts — 9 posts name several companies at once (one names 5), and those rows react to the same sentence at the same instant; (2) the ticker mix is concentrated — 12 of 73 are WBD alone; (3) same-day posts on one ticker share overlapping windows. Measurement floor: 5 observations show a first-minute move of exactly zero — on the low-priced names in this sample (WBD and PARA traded near $10–13) one tick is already ~0.08%, so "no move" is partly the price grid, not just calm. Those zeros sit exactly at the median, which is why the median first-minute move is +0.00%. The first-minute window straddles the post: the reference is the previous bar's close (up to ~60s before the post) and minute 0 is the post-minute's close (up to ~60s after), so it brackets the moment rather than starting exactly at it. This makes the "no reaction" finding conservative, not overstated. Past +30 min: a full +60 min window exists for 69 of 73 observations — mean +0.182% (median +0.207%, 61% hit rate), i.e. the drift continues rather than reverting, on an even thinner sample. Split: 31 praise / 42 attack — too thin to split the curve by sentiment with any confidence, so the headline curve combines them. No counterfactual: this measures the move around the post, not the move caused by it — there is no market/sector benchmark subtracted, and no control for news arriving at the same moment. A hit rate near 50% is what a coin flip looks like; the mean is carried by a few large moves, not by consistency.
